Somebody sits across a table and works through a list like that. They keep going after you have answered, because the first answer is almost never the whole one, and they do not stop when it gets uncomfortable. That is not cruelty. It is the job.

They are not trying to embarrass you. They are finding out what you do when somebody presses, while it is still a private room. The press will press too, and that room has cameras in it.

Vet yourself
Before they do

Seven sections, a hundred and forty prompts, and an honest inventory of everything in your past that could surface. You finish with a register: what to get ahead of, what to have written down, and what to simply know your answer to.

Your supporters can forgive almost anything except being surprised. That is the entire reason to do this. It only works if you are harder on yourself here than anybody else will be later, and you will want to stop somewhere around section four. Wanting to stop is not a sign that you should. It is the part of the exercise that tells you where the problem is.

At the top, they put you in a room

When a presidential campaign vets a running mate, or a transition vets someone for a cabinet job, it is not a form you fill in at your kitchen table. A team of lawyers takes years of your tax returns and your medical records. You complete a questionnaire. Then you sit down with them and they go through it line by line, and they keep going until the answers stop changing.

The questionnaire the Obama transition used in 2008 ran to more than sixty questions, and the New York Times published it in full. It asked for every email that might prove embarrassing. Every gun in the house. Every diary. Every alias you had ever posted under, and anything you had ever written, published or not. The federal security questionnaire behind a clearance is longer still and reaches back seven to ten years through your finances, your foreign contacts, your treatment history, and the people you live with.

Underneath all of it is one question, asked in various polite ways: is there anything about you, or anyone close to you, that could become a source of embarrassment. They are not asking to be cruel. They are asking because the alternative is finding out from a reporter.

Nobody does that for a school board race. That is exactly the problem. The scrutiny is smaller, but it is not zero, and it does not arrive with a team of lawyers and a schedule. It arrives on a Tuesday, from one person with a grudge and a search engine, six days before the election.

The people who do this work are not squeamish and they are not judging you. They ask in plain words because a euphemism is somewhere for an answer to hide, and a vetter who gets embarrassed is a vetter who misses things. A doctor does not apologise before an examination either.

This is the basic version of that room

What follows is a stripped-down, self-administered version of the same process: the same categories the professionals work through, in the same order, with a fixed rule at the end for deciding what to do about each thing you find. It is not the real thing, and it is worth being clear about what it cannot do.

  • It cannot pull your records. It does not see your court file, your credit report, or your property history. You have to go and get those.
  • It cannot interview anyone. Real vetting talks to your former business partner, your ex-spouse, and your neighbours. This only ever hears from you.
  • It does not know your disclosure law. What you are legally required to file, and when, is a question for your state and for a lawyer.
  • It cannot make you honest. It is a memory aid and a scoring rule. Everything it is worth depends on what you are willing to type into it.
  • It cannot press you. A real vetter hears your first answer, waits, and asks again. Nothing here will do that. When an answer comes out of you too quickly, that is the moment to stop and ask it yourself.

The prompts are the standard vetting scope plus a pass over what has actually ended careers in the last decade: fabricated resumes, intimate messages, old party photographs, relationships with people you had authority over, allegations and the settlements that quietly paid them, campaign money spent personally, and an afternoon at an event that was unremarkable on the day and notorious a year later.

That is still far more than most first-time candidates ever do, which is the entire point. Running opposition research on your own candidate is standard practice at every level of professional campaigning, and it is the first thing cut when there is no staff and no budget.

Nobody loses a race over a bankruptcy, an old arrest, or a post they wrote in 2014. They lose over the two weeks of coverage about why they never mentioned it.

Go and look. Do not answer from memory

The failure mode here is not lying. It is forgetting. The account you stopped using in 2014, the business that closed owing somebody money, the petition you signed outside a supermarket. Before you start, open the old accounts, search your own name, and read your own bio the way a hostile stranger would.

  • Search your own name in a private window, with your city, and with the words that would embarrass you.
  • Open every social account you have ever had, including ones you thought were deleted, and read the oldest posts first.
  • Pull your own records. County court records, property records, and your own voter history are public and mostly free.
  • Ask one person who will be blunt with you. You are the worst available judge of your own record.

You do not have to work out how to do any of that. The register at the end comes with the actual searches to run, written around your own name and town and tailored to whatever you flagged.

What it does with your answers

For everything you flag, you set two things: how likely it is to surface, and how bad it is if it does. Those two answers decide which of three actions applies, using a fixed rule rather than a judgement call, so the same inputs always produce the same register.

Severity is weighted ahead of likelihood on purpose. A severe item is worth pre-empting even when you are fairly sure nobody will find it, because the cost of being wrong about that is the campaign. Every rule traces back to published research, cited in the register.

Seven sections. Nothing is saved. You can stop and start over at any point, though the tool would rather you did not stop.
